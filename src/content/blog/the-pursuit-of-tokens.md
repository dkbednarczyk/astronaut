I am happy to admit that I'm the latest person to get hooked on Claude Code.
LLMs and their tooling at some point within the past year reached an
inflection point where for me they went from "hopefully it can figure this out" to
me adding "prompt engineer" to my resume. 

At some point, I started to get worried
about the balance between how much I typed in the editor and how much I typed
in the agent panel.

In talking with people outside of my technical bubble, I realized there's
a few distinct types of AI users, with little grey area between the different
levels.

There are those who oppose AI, whether for environmental, ethical, or personal
reasons. There are those who strive to use it productively,
as something that empowers what they already do day to day. I try to squeeze
myself into this category
even though it's admittedly hard to resist the "accept all edits" temptation.

What bothers me most is seeing how people interact with AI when they fall outside
both of these categories.

## Polarity

There's a new pattern I see all too often.

In the student center, I watch as a sophomore business administration student finds
an open seat and gets his laptop out. He pulls up his assignment on Canvas, and it's
a multiple choice quiz for his online class. He wasn't at the lecture today, but
that's no problem. He pulls out his phone, opens Snapchat of all things,
and starts snapping his [My AI](https://help.snapchat.com/hc/en-us/sections/13532188353428-My-AI) assistant pictures
of his screen and inputting whatever the AI says the answer is. 

He gets an 8/10
on the quiz, shrugs, and moves on. All he needs is a passing grade, and I don't think
he would put in the effort to learn the material anyway when he can just do the same thing
on the test. I probably wouldn't either if it was for a class I didn't care about.

I've seen this exact loop play out with my own eyes many times, and it makes me
start to vividly understand the hesitance to adopt AI in higher education. 
This behavior only becomes more rewarded as LLMs get better and more widely available.

This isn't even limited to Snapchat's AI either. Instagram had
an almost identical use case before they separated most of their AI features into
a separate app, and the plethora of free ChatGPT clones exacerbates the problem.

Behavior like this did not stem from people being taught about how to use LLMs 
productively. It came from AI features being shoved into every app with varying
qualities of implementation to the point where users either start to hate it,
try their hardest to ignore it, or abuse it.

This kind of behavior from companies trying to fit in with the latest AI trend
creates people who act as modern-day luddites. Those in younger
generations especially oppose these new advancements in AI, to the point
where [commencement speakers are being drowned by boos for
even mentioning it](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xwWaoyIy5e8).

But it also creates people who use any kind of advantage
they can get to stop needing to use their brains. Our student doesn't use, or even
know of, any tools besides his My AI and the ChatGPT app on his phone which he
shortens to "Chat" in normal conversation.

Next week is the first full week of May, finals week. He gets an email from his
professor about the final exam:

```
From: henderson@ilstu.edu  
To: BUS 201 — Introduction to Business (Spring 2026)  
Subject: Final exam information

Hello everyone,

As written in the syllabus, the final exam for BUS 201 will be held **in person**
this semester. It will be on the 6th, 1-3pm in Stevenson Hall room 131.

Please bring your student ID, and arrive at least 10 minutes early. 
No phones, no notes, no open-book. The exam will cover all material from weeks 1–14.

If you have a documented conflict or accommodation, contact me no later than Friday.

- Dr. Henderson 
```

Shit. A couple all-nighters ahead, I'm sure, as his class's groupchat blows up with
similar complaints and panicked messages. He will end up passing anyway after a
nailbiter 72% performance, since the professor was nice and it was easy enough
that as long as you showed up to lecture, you knew the answers. 

Wouldn't it be better if instead of him using AI as a crutch, it was
one of the most integral parts of his learning?

Those who use AI purely to delegate will just be slower than they
were before, with nothing left to lean on. Those who use it to think harder
will be miles ahead. The current pace and strategy of AI adoption unfortunately 
heavily discourages the latter.

I don't know where I'll eventually land with using Claude Code. Recently I've
tried to learn how its plugin systems work, and dabbled in multi-agent orchestration
(fancy term for what's really just prompting more than one thing at a time). But
self-reflecting: am I really learning something new or throwing away years of work
to have a tool replace it overnight? 

Chainsaws and excavators came for the jobs of men with axes and shovels.
Am I becoming the fabled blue collar tech worker?
